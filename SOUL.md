# SOUL — LongWatch

Three paragraphs, written before any code. They anchor every decision in this
round and every round after it.

## 1. Who is using this, and what hurts

Dana is a contract review analyst for a farm-certification body. It is 4pm on a
Thursday, her coffee has gone cold, and beside this browser sits a 4TB drive
holding a season of audit footage — about 200 hours — plus a spreadsheet of
facility IDs open on a laptop to her left. The certification panel meets Monday.
She has to hand them decisions she can defend line by line, and she has to make
them faster than real time, because watching 200 hours at 1× is not a job, it is
a sentence. What hurts is that every tool she has tried does one of two things:
it overstates (a red box labelled "suspicious" with no measurable reason
attached, which the panel shreds the moment she can't cite a number), or it
wastes her (a wall of thumbnails, a play button, and no ranking — the forty
spans that matter buried among four thousand that don't). She does not want a
verdict. She wants a queue that ranks by *measured* reasons, a record she can
point at months later, and a tool that never once says more than the data
supports.

## 2. What this feels like to use, hour 14 of 14

It feels like the readout bench of a phosphor instrument in a dark room: amber
traces on near-black, grid lines you can count, every value carrying its unit,
nothing on the panel that isn't measuring something. You stay in it for hours
because nothing moves under your hands — the row you are on stays where it is,
the number you just read does not re-render, the queue re-ranks quietly instead
of jumping, and the only thing that changes on screen is the thing you asked to
change. The lamp on the panel is the flag: the strongest verb in this interface
is "flag," and the brightest colour in the room belongs to it. It is a working
instrument, not a dashboard: it has a legend of keys printed along the bottom
edge, like a machine that expects you to learn it and then stop looking at the
chrome.

## 3. What it explicitly does NOT feel like

1. **Not a security-operations dashboard.** No threat map, no neon cyan on
   black, no radar sweep, no "THREAT LEVEL" banner. This product has no
   adversary and no live feed; it reads footage that already exists, on a
   machine that cannot call out. Surveillance aesthetics would contradict the
   product's refusals.
2. **Not a SaaS analytics dashboard.** No three KPI cards across the top, no
   hero stat, no gradient banner, no "Welcome back, Dana". Numbers live inside
   the surfaces that produced them, next to their units, never on a card
   designed to be screenshotted.
3. **Not a consumer video app.** No rounded thumbnail grid, no big red play
   button, no autoplay, no carousel. The player is an instrument pane, not an
   entertainment surface: it is the quietest bright thing on the screen and it
   never plays anything the reviewer did not ask for.
4. **Not television forensics.** No "ENHANCE", no red reticles over a frozen
   frame, no scanline overlay, no "MATCH FOUND". The product's whole ethical
   position is that it ranks spans and stops; the interface must be as boring
   and as exact as that claim, and every dramatic flourish would undercut it.
